# Chapter 26: Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

## 26.1 Why Mistakes Deserve a Full Chapter

Most trading failures do not come from never learning support and resistance. They come from applying a reasonable concept inconsistently: drawing too many zones, ignoring structure, entering without confirmation, risking too much, or changing rules after a loss. This chapter consolidates the most common errors in the SNR framework and gives each one a practical correction.

## 26.2 Drawing Too Many Zones

### The Mistake

A trader marks every visible swing, wick, and minor reaction until the chart is covered with rectangles. Every price becomes both support and resistance, so no setup can be ranked objectively.

### The Correction

Start with Daily/Weekly and mark only the most significant zones. Limit the active chart to a small number of major areas, then refine only the zone currently relevant to price. Require structural relevance, meaningful departure, and acceptable freshness before adding a zone.

## 26.3 Treating Zones as Exact Lines

### The Mistake

A trader expects reversal at one precise price and concludes the zone failed when price penetrates it slightly.

### The Correction

Use an area with inner and outer boundaries. Place stops beyond structural invalidation with a volatility-appropriate buffer. Judge whether price was accepted beyond the zone by candle closes and follow-through, not by a single wick.

## 26.4 Ignoring Higher-Timeframe Structure

### The Mistake

A trader buys every support zone or sells every resistance zone without checking whether the broader market is trending strongly in the opposite direction.

### The Correction

Apply the MSNR filter first. In an uptrend, prioritize support; in a downtrend, prioritize resistance. Counter-trend trades require stronger evidence, such as a confirmed MSS and clear multi-timeframe alignment.

## 26.5 Entering Without a Trigger

### The Mistake

Price touches the zone, and the trader enters immediately without evidence of rejection or control transfer.

### The Correction

Require a closed rejection candle, two-candle handoff, liquidity-sweep reversal, or LTF structural confirmation. If the move leaves without providing a trigger, accept the missed trade.

## 26.6 Confusing Every Wick with a Breakout

### The Mistake

A brief spike beyond support or resistance is treated as proof that the level has broken.

### The Correction

Check candle closure, follow-through, structural context, and whether price returns into the zone. A wick followed by a close back inside may be a liquidity sweep rather than genuine acceptance beyond the level.

## 26.7 Believing More Tests Always Strengthen a Zone

### The Mistake

The trader assumes a level is stronger because it has been touched many times.

### The Correction

Track freshness. Repeated tests can consume resting orders and weaken the reaction. Treat fresh or lightly tested zones as higher priority unless a role reversal or major higher-timeframe confluence changes the context.

## 26.8 Mixing Timeframes Without a Storyline

### The Mistake

The trader changes charts until finding a bullish or bearish reason to enter, creating bias-shopping and contradictory signals.

### The Correction

Assign each timeframe one job: HTF for bias and zones, MTF for approach and structural confirmation, LTF for entry timing. Maintain one storyline from the larger chart to the smaller chart.

## 26.9 Overusing Indicators

### The Mistake

The chart contains so many indicators that price action and SNR zones become secondary. The trader waits for every indicator to agree or takes trades solely because an oscillator is overbought or oversold.

### The Correction

Keep price action primary. Use a small number of indicators only as secondary confluence, and test whether each one improves expectancy after costs. Remove tools that add visual complexity without measurable benefit.

## 26.10 Treating SMC Terms as Automatic Confirmation

### The Mistake

A trader labels every candle an order block, every gap an FVG, and every wick a stop hunt. The vocabulary creates the illusion of precision without objective definitions.

### The Correction

Define each term mechanically and require it to fit structure, SNR location, and timeframe. SMC/ICT terminology should clarify price behavior, not replace analysis or become a collection of labels.

## 26.11 Moving Stops and Averaging Down

### The Mistake

After entry, the trader widens the stop to avoid a loss or adds to a losing position without a tested rule.

### The Correction

Place the stop at structural invalidation before entry. If the trade invalidates, accept the planned loss. Any scaling process must be predefined, total risk must remain capped, and averaging down must never be used to rescue an untested idea.

## 26.12 Risking Too Much on High-Confidence Setups

### The Mistake

A setup with several confluences is treated as nearly certain, so the trader exceeds the normal risk limit.

### The Correction

Confluence improves selection but does not eliminate uncertainty. Use confluence to choose among setups or apply a tested sizing tier, never to justify unlimited risk. Keep daily, weekly, and correlated-exposure limits active.

## 26.13 Chasing Price and FOMO

### The Mistake

The trader enters after a breakout has already traveled far from the zone or opens a position because other traders appear to be profiting.

### The Correction

Define a maximum acceptable distance from the zone. If the planned entry is gone, wait for a valid retest or new setup. Your strategy does not need to capture every move to be profitable.

## 26.14 Ignoring News, Spread, and Execution

### The Mistake

A technically attractive setup is traded immediately before major news, during abnormal spread conditions, or with incorrect contract-value assumptions.

### The Correction

Check the economic calendar, current spread, liquidity, and broker specifications. If the execution environment violates the plan, skip or reduce exposure according to pretested rules.

## 26.15 Changing the Strategy After a Small Sample

### The Mistake

One or two losses lead to new filters, new indicators, or a completely different entry model.

### The Correction

Classify the trades, review a meaningful sample, and compare results with the expected distribution. Change one rule at a time and validate it on new data. Avoid optimizing the strategy around memorable chart examples.

## 26.16 Failing to Journal Skipped Trades

### The Mistake

The trader records only executed positions and ignores valid setups that were skipped or missed.

### The Correction

Record no-trade opportunities with the same context fields: zone, bias, trigger, reason for skipping, and subsequent outcome. This helps reveal whether your filters are too strict, appropriately selective, or being applied inconsistently.

## 26.17 A Final Pre-Trade Audit

Before any live order, ask:

- What is the HTF market state?
- Where is the qualified SNR zone?
- Is it fresh or tested?
- Does MSNR favor this direction?
- What is the exact LTF trigger?
- Where is structural invalidation?
- What is the monetary risk after spread and commission?
- What are the logical targets?
- Is there event, spread, correlation, or platform risk?
- Would I still take this trade if I had no knowledge of the next candle?

If the answer to a required question is unclear, do not force the trade. Clarity is part of the setup.

## 26.18 Final Case Example

XAUUSD reaches a resistance zone during a Daily downtrend. The trader sees a bearish rejection candle and wants to sell. The zone, however, has already been tested four times, the H1 structure has shifted bullish, and a major US release is imminent. The trader skips the setup despite the attractive candle.

This decision avoids three mistakes at once: ignoring freshness, ignoring MTF structure, and ignoring event risk. Whether price later falls or rises does not change the quality of the decision at the moment it was made.

## 26.19 Chapter Summary

- Most SNR failures come from inconsistent application rather than lack of knowledge.
- Keep charts selective, treat zones as areas, and let HTF structure guide direction.
- Require a trigger, distinguish sweeps from breakouts, and track freshness.
- Avoid indicator overload, vague SMC labels, stop widening, revenge trading, and FOMO.
- Respect news, spread, contract specifications, correlated exposure, and platform reliability.
- Judge decisions using information available before the outcome, then improve rules through meaningful data.
