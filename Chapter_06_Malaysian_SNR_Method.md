# Chapter 6: The Malaysian SNR Method

## 6.1 Origins and Purpose

The "Malaysian SNR" method is a widely taught variation of support and resistance analysis that emerged from forex trading communities in Malaysia and has since spread across Southeast Asian retail trading circles. Unlike the candlestick body-marking approach in Chapter 5, this method uses a **line chart** to strip away candle noise entirely and focus purely on closing-price structure. It's a complementary tool, not a replacement — many traders cross-check zones from both methods for extra confidence.

## 6.2 Why Switch to a Line Chart

A line chart connects only closing prices, removing the visual clutter of wicks and bodies. Proponents of this method argue that since the close is the price both buyers and sellers "agreed" on by the end of a session, it's the most meaningful single data point for structural analysis. This is a deliberately different philosophy from Chapter 5's body-based zones — think of it as a second lens for confirming the same underlying levels.

## 6.3 The "V" Shape (Support Identification)

On a line chart, a **V shape** forms when price descends, hits a low point, and reverses upward — visually resembling the letter V. The bottom point of this V is marked as a potential support level. Key rules:

- The V must show a clear, sharp turning point — not a rounded, gradual bottom.
- The sharper and more symmetrical the V, the stronger the implied support.
- Multiple V shapes at similar price levels (across different time periods) reinforce the strength of that support zone, consistent with the "number of touches" discussion in Chapter 3.

## 6.4 The "A" Shape (Resistance Identification)

Mirroring the V, an **A shape** (or inverted V) forms when price ascends, peaks, and reverses downward. The peak of the A marks a potential resistance level. The same sharpness and symmetry rules apply — a rounded, gradual top suggests weaker resistance than a sharp spike-and-reject A shape.

## 6.5 Step-by-Step: Malaysian SNR Marking

1. Switch your chart type to line (closing prices only), on your analysis timeframe (Daily/H4).
2. Scan for clear V and A shapes across recent price history.
3. Mark horizontal levels at each V (support) and A (peak) point.
4. Switch back to candlesticks and check whether these line-chart levels align with the body-based zones from Chapter 5.
5. Where both methods agree, you have a **high-confidence zone** — this convergence is one of the simplest and most powerful confluence checks in the entire book.

## 6.6 Strengths and Limitations

**Strengths:**
- Extremely fast to apply visually — good for quickly scanning multiple charts (useful if you're monitoring several pairs or gold alongside other instruments).
- Removes wick noise entirely, which can help beginners avoid the common mistake of overreacting to long wicks.

**Limitations:**
- Loses information about intra-period volatility and rejection strength (which the body/wick method in Chapter 5 captures).
- Can be more subjective — what counts as a "clear" V or A shape varies between traders, whereas the two-candle handoff rule in Chapter 5 is more mechanically precise.
- Works best as a **confirmation tool** alongside candlestick-based SNR, not as a sole method for entries.

## 6.7 Worked Example

Suppose the XAUUSD Daily line chart shows price declining from $2,450 to a sharp low at $2,300, then reversing to $2,400 — a clear V at $2,300. Separately, using the Chapter 5 method on the candlestick chart, you find a bearish→bullish handoff zone at $2,298–$2,303. These two independently derived levels nearly overlap, giving you a high-confidence support zone at approximately $2,300 — exactly the kind of convergence discussed in Section 6.5.

## 6.8 Chapter Summary

- The Malaysian SNR method uses line charts and V/A shapes to identify support and resistance from closing prices alone.
- Sharp, symmetrical V and A shapes indicate stronger zones; rounded shapes indicate weaker ones.
- This method works best as a cross-check against the candlestick body-based method from Chapter 5.
- Convergence between both methods at a similar price produces high-confidence zones worth prioritizing.
