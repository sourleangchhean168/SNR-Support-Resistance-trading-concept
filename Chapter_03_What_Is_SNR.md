# Chapter 3: What Is SNR (Support & Resistance)?

## 3.1 Formal Definition

**SNR (Support and Resistance)** refers to price zones where the market has historically reversed, paused, or accelerated due to a shift in the balance between buyers and sellers.

- **Support** is a price zone where buying pressure has previously overwhelmed selling pressure, halting or reversing a decline.
- **Resistance** is a price zone where selling pressure has previously overwhelmed buying pressure, halting or reversing an advance.

The critical word in both definitions is **zone**, not "line." A single-pixel line on a chart implies mathematical precision that doesn't exist in real markets. Price rarely reverses at the exact same price twice — it reverses within a *range* of prices clustered around a psychologically or institutionally significant level. This is the first major shift in thinking every SNR trader must make, and it will be reinforced throughout Chapter 8.

## 3.2 Support and Resistance Are the Same Thing, Just Inverted

A crucial insight for this book: support and resistance are not two separate phenomena — they are the same mechanism (an imbalance of orders at a price) viewed from opposite directions. This is why, as you'll see in Chapter 9, a broken support zone frequently *becomes* resistance, and a broken resistance zone frequently *becomes* support. Understanding SNR as a single unified concept, rather than two unrelated rules, will make every later chapter — especially MSNR in Part 3 — much easier to apply.

## 3.3 The Psychology Behind SNR (Expanded from Chapter 1)

Chapter 1 introduced three psychological drivers behind SNR. Let's apply them directly to a price zone:

1. **Regret and breakeven-seeking.** Imagine 10,000 retail traders bought EURUSD at 1.0850, and price fell to 1.0800. When price recovers back to 1.0850, many of these traders sell just to exit at breakeven — creating fresh selling pressure exactly at that price. This zone now behaves as resistance.
2. **FOMO re-entry.** Traders who missed the original bounce off a support zone will buy aggressively the second time price approaches it, reinforcing the level.
3. **Institutional replenishment.** A bank trying to buy 50,000 lots may only fill 30,000 at a given zone. When price returns, the remaining unfilled 20,000-lot order still sits there, causing price to react again.

These forces don't disappear after one test — which is why SNR zones can remain relevant for weeks or months, especially on higher timeframes (Daily/Weekly).

## 3.4 SNR vs. Indicator-Based Support/Resistance

Many beginners confuse SNR with indicator-drawn levels like moving averages, Fibonacci retracements, or pivot points. These are **derived** levels — calculated from formulas. SNR, as taught in this book, is **price-action-derived** — it comes directly from where actual reversals occurred on the chart, with no calculation involved. This doesn't mean indicators are useless (Chapter 12 covers using them as confluence), but they are never the primary basis for a valid SNR zone in this methodology.

## 3.5 Strong vs. Weak SNR Zones

Not all SNR zones carry equal weight. Several factors increase a zone's strength:

- **Timeframe** — a zone on the Weekly chart is inherently stronger than the same price level identified only on M15.
- **Number of touches (with a caveat)** — a zone tested once and holding strongly is often *more* reliable than one tested five times, because each touch consumes some of the liquidity/orders resting there. This "freshness" concept is covered in depth in Chapter 7.
- **Speed of the original move away from the zone** — a sharp, impulsive departure from a level indicates strong imbalance and a high-quality zone; a slow, grinding departure suggests a weaker zone.
- **Confluence with structure** — zones that align with a clear swing high/low (Chapter 2) or a market structure shift (Chapter 4) are considered higher quality.

## 3.6 A Simple Example

Picture XAUUSD rallying strongly off $2,350, running up to $2,420, then pulling back. According to SNR logic:

- $2,350 becomes a **support zone** because it's where the sharp reversal began.
- If price later breaks below $2,350 and then returns to test it from underneath, $2,350 now behaves as a **resistance zone** (role reversal, detailed in Chapter 9).
- The strength of this zone depends on the timeframe it was drawn on, how sharply price left it, and whether it's still "fresh" (untested since formation).

This single example demonstrates the entire SNR philosophy in miniature — and every remaining chapter in Part 2 builds directly on it.

## 3.7 Chapter Summary

- SNR zones are areas, not lines, where buyer/seller imbalance previously caused a reversal.
- Support and resistance are the same underlying mechanism viewed in opposite directions, which explains role reversal (Chapter 9).
- The psychology of regret, FOMO, and institutional replenishment explains why these zones remain relevant over time.
- SNR is price-action-derived, distinct from indicator-based levels like Fibonacci or pivots.
- Zone strength depends on timeframe, freshness, speed of departure, and structural confluence.
