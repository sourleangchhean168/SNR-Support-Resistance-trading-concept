# Chapter 1: Introduction to Markets and Price Action

## 1.1 Why Price Moves

Every price you see on a chart — whether it's XAUUSD, EURUSD, or any other instrument — is the result of a battle between buyers and sellers. Price does not move because of "indicators" or "patterns." It moves because of **order flow**: real buy and sell orders hitting the market. Indicators and patterns are just visual tools that help us *interpret* where that order flow is likely concentrated.

Three forces drive every price movement:

- **Supply and demand imbalance** — when buy orders outweigh sell orders at a price, price rises; when sell orders outweigh buy orders, price falls.
- **Liquidity** — large institutional players (banks, hedge funds, market makers) need liquidity (opposing orders) to fill their large positions. They often target areas where retail stop-losses and pending orders cluster.
- **Market psychology** — fear, greed, and herd behavior cause price to overreact at certain levels, creating the sharp reversals we call support and resistance.

Understanding this is essential before you touch SNR (Support and Resistance) trading, because SNR zones are not magic lines — they are footprints left behind by real buying and selling pressure.

## 1.2 Who Moves the Market

- **Retail traders** — individuals like you and me. Individually small, but collectively influential, especially through predictable behavior (tight stop-losses, obvious entries).
- **Institutional traders / smart money** — banks, hedge funds, and market makers who move large volumes and often need retail liquidity to execute their orders efficiently. This is the foundation of Smart Money Concepts (SMC) and ICT methodology, which we'll integrate with SNR later in the book.
- **Market makers** — entities that provide liquidity and, in doing so, often create the wicks and false breakouts that trap retail traders at SNR zones.

Recognizing that "someone" is always on the other side of your trade changes how you view every support and resistance zone: it's not just a line, it's a location where large volumes of past orders exist and future orders are likely to react.

## 1.3 Price Action — The Native Language of the Market

Price action refers to reading raw price movement on a chart, without lagging indicators, to understand market intent. It is the foundation of SNR trading because SNR zones are entirely price-action-based — they come from where price previously reversed, not from a mathematical formula.

Key price action concepts you must know before proceeding:

- **Bullish move** — a sustained sequence of higher highs and higher lows.
- **Bearish move** — a sustained sequence of lower highs and lower lows.
- **Consolidation/range** — price oscillating between two levels without clear directional bias.
- **Impulse vs. correction** — an impulse is a strong, fast move in the trend direction; a correction is a retracement against it. SNR zones are usually formed at the *start* of an impulse.

## 1.4 Why Support and Resistance Exists

Support and resistance exist because markets have memory. Traders who bought at a certain price and regretted it will often sell again if price returns there ("get me out at breakeven"). Traders who missed a bounce will buy again if price returns to that same level ("I'll buy the dip this time"). This repeated psychological behavior is why price tends to react at the same zones repeatedly — and it's the entire basis for the SNR concept you'll master in this book.

Three specific psychological patterns create SNR levels:

1. **Regret and breakeven-seeking** — traders trapped in a bad position exit as soon as price returns to their entry, creating a wall of selling (resistance) or buying (support).
2. **Fear of missing out (FOMO)** — traders who missed a big move jump in when price retests a "proven" zone, reinforcing it.
3. **Institutional order replenishment** — large players who didn't fully fill their orders the first time will place remaining orders at the same zone, causing price to react there again.

## 1.5 Setting Up Your Charts

Before you can identify SNR zones (covered fully in Chapter 5), set up your charting platform correctly:

- Use **candlestick charts**, not line charts, for identifying wicks and bodies (we'll explain why bodies matter more than wicks for SNR in Chapter 5).
- Start your SNR analysis on the **Daily and H4 timeframes** for the "big picture" zones, then drop to H1/M15 for entries (covered in Chapter 11: Multi-Timeframe Analysis).
- Keep your charts clean. A common mistake beginners make is drawing 20+ SNR lines on one chart, which destroys clarity (we address this fully in Chapter 26: Common Mistakes).

## 1.6 Chapter Summary

- Price moves due to real order flow driven by supply/demand, liquidity needs, and psychology — not by indicators alone.
- Institutional players often need retail liquidity, which explains why price frequently reverses sharply at "obvious" levels.
- Price action — reading raw candles and structure — is the foundation for identifying valid SNR zones.
- SNR levels exist because of trader psychology: regret, FOMO, and institutional order replenishment.
- Clean, timeframe-appropriate charts are essential before you begin marking SNR zones.
